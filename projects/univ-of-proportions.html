A Universe of Proportions

Venue: University of Central Oklahoma
Date: 2018
Audience: Physics & Engineering Students
Download: ⬇

What is the universal language that connects a spiral galaxy to a seashell, Kepler's laws to musical harmony, Newton's second law to the stability of bridges?

Overview

What unites a spiral galaxy, a seashell, a stock market chart, and the structure of DNA? What connects Kepler's laws of planetary motion to the tuning of musical instruments, Archimedes' principle to the efficiency of machines, Newton's second law to the stability of bridges? The answer is proportion—the fundamental relationship between quantities that reveals the deepest patterns in nature and thought.

This conceptual piece explores how ratios and proportions form a universal language underlying physics, engineering, biology, chemistry, mathematics, and even music and art. The thesis is elegant: anything dimensionally equivalent can be expressed as a ratio, and understanding these proportional relationships reveals the structure of reality itself.

Analogies and Cognition

At the core of human cognition lies analogy—comparison between two forms that share a property. The word "analogy" originates from the Greek word for proportion, and its logical form is immediate: "A is to B as C is to D." This proportional thinking is not merely a rhetorical device; it is how we understand new phenomena by relating them to familiar ones, how we recognize patterns across domains, and how we make inferences about an unknown world based on observations of what we know.

Historical Development

Humans have formalized proportional thinking for millennia. The Babylonians recorded ratios of right triangle sides on clay tablets (Plimpton 322, ~1800 BC). Egyptians developed the concept of "seked" (slope) and estimated π as 256/81 (Rhind Papyrus, ~1550 BC). Pythagoras systematized the theory of proportions around 500 BC. By the 9th century, Al-Khwarizmi had tabulated sine, cosine, and tangent functions—themselves ratios of geometric projections. This progression from observation to formalization to application spans millennia yet reveals a consistent theme: proportions are how we understand and control the physical world.

From Ratios to Equations

Proportions are more than static relationships. They connect to dynamics through differential calculus: when proportions are not linear but "ratios of ratios," we obtain equations that describe change itself. Newton's second law (F = ma), Kepler's third law (relating orbital period to distance), Pascal's principle (pressure transmits equally), and the Doppler effect (frequency shift with velocity) are all fundamentally proportional relationships—specific expressions of the ratio principle applied to dynamics and measurement.

Universal Application

The work catalogs proportional relationships across domains: physics (speed of light, friction coefficients, indices of refraction, spring stiffness), chemistry (stoichiometry, reaction rates, molar masses, gas laws), biology (genetic ratios, surface-area-to-volume ratios, population growth, photosynthesis intensity), mathematics (golden ratio, Fibonacci sequences, planetary distances), and statistics (percent error, confidence intervals). The range reveals a stunning fact: the proportional method works everywhere. Whether analyzing why a tree cannot grow infinitely large, how electrical resistance scales with wire dimensions, or why certain musical intervals sound consonant, the answer involves ratios.

The Core Insight

The central claim is deceptively simple: anything can be expressed as a ratio so long as the compared parameters are dimensionally equivalent. But this simplicity masks a profound truth about the structure of knowledge itself. Ratios are easy to create—any two quantities can be divided. Proportionalities—meaningful equality of ratios that reveal deep structure—are difficult to establish. Discovering proportionalities is the work of science, engineering, and mathematics. Understanding why certain ratios persist across different phenomena, and why others do not, is the path to comprehension.
