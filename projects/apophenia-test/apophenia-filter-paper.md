# The Apophenia Filter

## An Instrument for Separating Discrimination from Luck in Sequential Prediction — and the Record of Its Own Design

**Cole Prather (Peirastes)**
*Working paper, v1.0 — July 2026*
*Drafted collaboratively with AI assistance; the intellectual history recorded here is the actual development record.*

---

### Abstract

This paper documents the design of a behavioral instrument — the Apophenia Filter — that measures a human or machine observer's capacity to discriminate genuine structure from noise in sequential prediction, and it documents the reasoning that transformed the project from its original purpose into its final one. The project began as a proposal to predict financial markets, motivated by the author's felt sense that he could recognize patterns at multiple timescales. It ended as an instrument whose first substantive act was to classify its own inventor as apophenic. Along the way, the design absorbed and operationalized several threads: Feynman's distinction between learning the rules of a system and knowing its author; the formal gap between determinism and predictability; the survivorship problem that makes forecasting advice unfalsifiable when sampled from winners; the observation that trend extrapolation is always an implicit mechanism claim; and a teaching-assessment framework in which exams are treated as diagnostic filters with embedded self-checks rather than as gates. The resulting instrument scores calibration rather than accuracy, treats abstention as a first-class skilled action, embeds sentinel trials that test the instrument as much as the subject, and — critically — is tuned against Monte Carlo oracle ceilings rather than against any human's performance. The tuning process itself produced the paper's first empirical results: two silent design failures were caught by simulation before a single human session was collected, and one mechanism class (momentum) was found to have an intrinsic predictability ceiling of roughly 88% at the task horizon regardless of signal strength. We argue that the instrument's chief contribution is not any verdict it renders on a participant but the epistemic form it enforces: a prediction claim is admissible only as a calibration record against a known ceiling, produced under disclosure, with the instrument's own validity independently checked.

### 1. Origin: a prediction machine, inverted

The project began with a familiar ambition, honestly held. The author believed he could recognize patterns in market movements at different timescales, and reasoned that a machine could parse the same information faster and more thoroughly, returning a recommendation — buy, sell, hold, wait — with quantified uncertainty. The framing device was borrowed from Feynman: an observer watching chess can learn the rules without ever knowing who invented the game. Imagine, then, a ball suspended in fluid, driven up and down by variable forces — random to an uninformed observer, deterministic to an omniscient one, probabilistic to a partially informed one. If markets are such a ball, one ought to be able to learn their rules phenomenologically, without any ontology of *why* the forces exist.

The formal skeleton of that image is standard. The ball is a one-dimensional stochastic process — a Langevin equation with time-varying drift — and the hidden force mechanism is a latent generative process. The three observers correspond to three information conditions: the omniscient observer holds the full state vector and dynamics; the uninformed observer sees only a martingale, irreducibly random *to them*; the partially informed observer performs Bayesian filtering, maintaining a posterior over latent states given the observable trajectory. This is precisely the territory of hidden Markov models, regime-switching processes, and Kalman or particle filtering. Nothing in the framing was new. What proved consequential was a constraint the framing makes visible: **determinism and predictability come apart.** In a chaotic deterministic system, two trajectories from almost identical initial states diverge exponentially at a rate set by the Lyapunov exponent; since any real observer measures state at finite precision, forecast error grows until, past a horizon of roughly (1/λ)·ln(1/ε), prediction is no better than sampling the system's long-run distribution — even though the dynamics contain no randomness at all. Weather forecasting dies at two weeks for this reason, independent of compute. Whatever "learning the market" could mean, it has a finite justified window even in principle.

The decisive turn, however, was not mathematical but epistemological, and it was self-directed. The founding claim — "I feel like I can recognize patterns" — is exactly the kind of claim the proposed system would need to adjudicate, and it is a claim that introspection cannot settle. Humans are exceptional pattern detectors with a documented failure mode: we cannot *feel* the difference between detecting structure and hallucinating it. Shown pure random walks, people confidently identify trends, support levels, and reversals; the visual texture of a martingale is indistinguishable, at human perceptual resolution, from the visual texture of weak structure. The honest null hypothesis was therefore that the author's felt pattern recognition was apophenia on noise. Under the discipline this research program imposes on itself — locate yourself in the problem before solving it — the first observer the instrument had to test was its inventor. The prediction machine inverted into a falsification machine, and the market demoted itself from target to eventual, uncontrolled test case.

### 2. Why the market cannot be the first test case

Two properties disqualify market data as the proving ground, and both became design principles.

The first is the unfalsifiability of backtests. A strategy that "works" on historical data cannot be distinguished, from the data alone, from an overfit description of noise: there is no answer key. A testbed with *embedded* known mechanisms supplies the answer key. By generating trajectories from mechanisms we specify — a persistent drift, a mean-reverting pull, a hidden regime that flips — recovery becomes decidable. One can measure an observer's inference capacity against ground truth first, and only then point the calibrated observer at real markets and ask what it finds. This is Conant–Ashby made operational: every good regulator of a system must contain a model of that system, and with ground truth in hand one can check whether it does.

The second is reflexivity. The ball's forces do not care that they are being watched; markets do. The moment an observer acts on a learned pattern, they perturb the mechanism that generated it — Goodhart's law meeting the adaptive-markets hypothesis. It is tempting to analogize this to quantum measurement, and the analogy was raised and rejected during design: the market observer effect is game-theoretic — agents strategically adapting to being exploited — not anything like measurement collapse, and conflating the two is a classic route to dismissal. The honest statement is that the instrument's transfer to markets is bounded by reflexivity, and that an eventual adversarial mode — in which the generative mechanism adapts against successful predictors, converting the physics problem into a two-player game — is the correct way to study that boundary rather than to wave at it.

A corollary worth stating plainly, because it disciplines expectations: as a trading edge, this program is nearly worthless. The exploitable structure in modern markets is thin, decays under exploitation, and is contested by institutions with decades of head start. As an epistemological instrument — a device that measures *what is learnable in principle from this data, by this observer* — it is a genuine contribution, and the likely market-data result ("most of what looks like structure is not recoverable at retail observation budgets") is itself a publishable, falsification-flavored finding rather than a failure.

### 3. The lucky forecaster, and why the score must be calibration

Two threads from outside the project converged on its scoring rule.

The first is an aphorism: *the only thing more dangerous than a forecaster is a lucky forecaster.* Its mechanism is survivorship compounded by multiple comparisons. Run a large ensemble of guessers through a short sequence of binary calls and some go undefeated by construction; those survivors now hold "proof," and they are disproportionately the ones interviewed, published, and imitated. During the project's development, a widely shared piece of forecasting advice was examined in exactly this light. Its content — that weird-sounding outcomes are penalized socially rather than probabilistically, and that trend extrapolation is underused rather than overused — may well be sound. But its *frame* was an interviewer asking a successful forecaster how he got to be so good: a question already conditioned on success. Advice sampled from winners cannot be distinguished, by the listener, from luck teaching itself as method. The resolution is not cynicism — the superforecasting literature demonstrates that persistent, identifiable skill exists — but a change of admissible evidence. The only valid answer to "are you a good forecaster" is a calibration record across many registered, scored predictions. A Brier score, not a narrative.

The second thread came from the author's earlier research on nonlinear population growth, which instilled a wariness the extrapolation advice seemed to flout. The reconciliation is that "extrapolate a trend as far forward as it extends back" is a *persistence heuristic*, and persistence is an implicit mechanism claim: that whatever drives the trend has inertia on the relevant timescale and no constraint boundary nearby. A logistic curve is spectrally indistinguishable from an exponential until the knee, and trend data alone cannot certify the constraint's absence. Interpolation is bounded by data on both sides; extrapolation imports a model of the generator whether or not one admits it. Both intuitions are therefore correct in their domains — extrapolation earns its keep where drivers have known inertia and fails catastrophically near carrying capacities — and even a real trend carries a finite justified horizon. For the instrument, the lesson generalized: every prediction the participant registers is an implicit mechanism claim, and the scoring must price the confidence of that claim, not merely its direction.

Hence the instrument's core rule. Participants do not simply call up or down; they attach a confidence, and every call is scored by the Brier rule, (p − outcome)². A perpetual abstainer — one who claims 50% on everything — scores exactly 0.250. Overconfident randomness bleeds points against that benchmark; genuine discrimination beats it; and luck, which does not compound, regresses toward it. Repetition is the arbiter, mathematically. The instrument does not argue with the confident; it lets them refute themselves — peirastic dialectic, automated.

### 4. The instrument as filter: importing an assessment architecture

The design crystallized when it was recognized as an instance of an architecture the author had already built for a different faculty: an exam framework in which assessments are treated as measurement instruments rather than gates, governed by the question *"what does this score tell me — and is the exam measuring the student, or measuring itself?"* That framework stages difficulty deliberately (hard, hard, easy) so that comparison *across* stages isolates single variables: challenge problems separate understanding from memorization; an easy, fully signposted exam separates effort from apathy. Its diagnostic payoff is a set of cross-stage profiles, and its two guardrails are a fairness constraint — students must be told what to study, or a low score on the easy exam is uninterpretable — and a self-check: an individual's flat performance on well-signposted material is an effort signal, but a *class-wide* collapse points back at the exam.

Every element ports. The Filter's pure-noise trials are the easy exam: they isolate one variable — restraint, or calibration — because on a martingale the only correct behavior is to withhold conviction. Without them, a genuine discriminator and a lucky guesser are indistinguishable in a short session; with them, conviction spent on nothing becomes a measured quantity, the **apophenia index**. The disclosure screen is the fairness constraint elevated to an invariant: before any trial, participants are told the approximate noise proportion, the mechanism families and their visual tells, that abstention is a legitimate scored action, and that deliberately obvious trials exist. Absent that disclosure, low abstention could mean "didn't know folding was allowed" rather than apophenia, and the signal is void. Sentinel trials — high-signal trajectories that any attentive observer should catch — are the self-check: an individual missing sentinels is an attention or perception signal, but a population missing them indicts the instrument's calibration, not the subjects. The class-wide-jump rule, inverted.

The participant profiles fall out as a quadrant classification. **A, Discriminator:** sensitive to embedded structure, restrained on martingales — the profile the instrument exists to find, and the one luck cannot sustain. **B, Calibrated-but-insensitive:** conviction correctly withheld everywhere, structure unrecovered — honest and trainable; the limiting factor is perception, not judgment. **C, Undecidable:** hits on structure but conviction also spent on noise — real perception contaminated by apophenia, or luck; unresolvable at small n, by design labeled rather than adjudicated. **D, Apophenic:** conviction everywhere, discrimination nowhere — the default human profile on financial charts. A fifth outcome, Unclassifiable, is returned when too few committed calls exist to measure anything: the filter needs conviction to measure, and universal abstention, while calibrated, carries no discrimination signal.

One reframing deserves emphasis because it recovers the project's origin. The original prediction machine was to output buy, sell, hold, or *wait*. The instrument's deepest finding about skill is that the highest behavior it can detect is the fourth option — knowing when not to predict. The "wait" action survived the inversion intact; everything else was scaffolding around it.

### 5. Implementation

The deployed instrument (v0.4) is a single-file browser application. Each trial presents sixty steps of a trajectory — rendered simultaneously as a strip-chart trace and as a ball suspended in a fluid chamber, honoring the founding image — and asks where the ball will be twelve steps later: higher, lower, or *no edge*, with committed calls carrying a confidence between 55% and 95%. The future then resolves step by step, the trial is Brier-scored, and the next trajectory is dealt. Mechanisms remain hidden until a final report, which delivers the quadrant classification, core metrics, a calibration table (claimed confidence versus realized accuracy), the apophenia index, the sentinel check, and a full reveal of every trial's generating mechanism.

Four mechanism classes populate the deck. Pure noise is a Gaussian random walk — a martingale with nothing to find. Momentum is an autoregressive drift: recent slope tends to persist. Mean reversion is an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck pull toward an anchor, with (at higher signal tiers) a shock injected shortly before the prediction point so that recoverable displacement actually exists when the question is asked — a fairness fix, since a mean-reverting process resting at its anchor is unpredictable by construction, and asking about it is an unfair question. Regime switching is a hidden two-state drift that flips sign at random intervals. A full session deals twenty-four trials, roughly 40% noise, with structured trials distributed across three signal tiers; a quick session deals twelve.

Every trajectory is generated from a stored pseudorandom seed, making it exactly reproducible from the tuple (seed, mechanism, tier, instrument version). This is the spine of the data architecture: it permits any later observer — an ideal Bayesian filter, a Kalman filter, a neural network — to be scored on *the same trajectories* a human faced, trial for trial, rather than on the mechanism class in aggregate. Sessions log per-trial response times, an instrument version, and a parameter hash that changes with any retuning, so that sessions from different tunings can never be pooled silently.

### 6. Tuning against ground truth: the oracle ceiling method

The gravest methodological trap in the deployment plan was named before it was sprung: the intention to "calibrate the instrument on myself, then share it." Tuning signal strengths until the inventor's results look sensible calibrates the instrument to one person's perceptual floor, after which every other participant's data measures difference-from-the-inventor rather than discrimination. The fix is to tune against ground truth ceilings, computed offline, with no humans in the loop at all.

For each mechanism and tier, a Monte Carlo harness (N = 30,000 trials per cell) measures the accuracy of an **oracle observer** — one granted the latent state at the moment of prediction: the current drift value, the current regime, the current displacement — applying the sign-optimal decision rule. The oracle's accuracy is the physical ceiling of the task: the fraction of trials on which even perfect inference of the hidden state yields a correct directional call, the remainder being genuinely unknowable future noise. A practical observer using only history (exponentially weighted slope estimates; window-mean anchor estimates) is measured alongside, bounding what an unaided but competent human could approach.

The harness's first run, against the instrument's provisional parameters, produced the project's first empirical results — both of them instrument failures caught before any human data existed. First, momentum's tier staging was fake: scaling the drift's driving noise scales the predictable signal and the unpredictable future innovations together, so oracle accuracy was nearly flat (68% to 70%) across a threefold nominal signal range, and the "sentinel" tier had a ceiling of 70% — meaning a perfect observer misses it three times in ten, which is no sentinel at all. A population "failing" that trial would have wrongly indicted the participants. Second, mean reversion was trivially easy even at its lowest tier (89% ceiling), because the injected shock dominated the dynamics. Both failures were invisible to inspection and to casual play; both were unambiguous in simulation.

Parameter sweeps identified the correct staging levers: momentum stages *persistence* (the drift's memory, φ) rather than its amplitude; mean reversion stages the spring constant and shock size jointly; regime switching stages drift magnitude against switch probability. The tuned parameter set, with measured oracle ceilings:

| Mechanism | Subtle | Standard | Sentinel |
|---|---|---|---|
| Momentum (stage φ; innovation 0.25, obs. noise 0.7) | φ = 0.82 → 63% | φ = 0.92 → 73% | φ = 0.99 → 88% |
| Mean reversion (stage k, shock at t = 56) | k = 0.04, no shock → 65% | k = 0.12, shock 1.5 → 73% | k = 0.12, shock 8 → 93% |
| Regime (stage μ, switch prob. p) | μ = 0.30, p = 0.06 → 66% | μ = 0.45, p = 0.04 → 77% | μ = 0.90, p = 0.01 → 94% |

One result in that table is a finding rather than a choice. Momentum's ceiling saturates near 88% at this twelve-step horizon: even with near-permanent drift (φ = 0.99), observation noise flips the realized outcome often enough that no observer, however perfect, can exceed it. Momentum is intrinsically the hardest structure class at this horizon, and the instrument now says so — the report displays every participant's tier accuracy *against its ceiling*, and the disclosure screen tells participants in advance that even perfection cannot always be right. The gap between a participant and the ceiling is the recoverable-but-unrecovered signal; the gap between the ceiling and 100% is the Lyapunov lesson, made concrete and shown to every player.

### 7. Subject zero

The inventor took the test first. He got a D.

The result deserves exactly the treatment the instrument prescribes and no more. It is a single session of modest length, with wide error bars, and — decisively — it was earned on the pre-retuning prototype whose momentum sentinel was later shown to be nearly impossible and whose tier structure was partly illusory. The letter is therefore provisional pending retrial on the tuned instrument. But its epistemic function is already discharged: the founding intuition, "I feel like I can recognize patterns at different timescales," took a direct falsification hit at a cost of twenty minutes rather than a brokerage account, and the project's inversion — from prediction machine to self-directed filter — was vindicated in its first use. If the retrial upholds the grade, the paper's first data point writes its own discussion section. If it does not, the difference between the two sessions is itself a measurement of what the retuning changed. Either branch produces knowledge; that is what distinguishes an instrument from a toy.

### 8. Data architecture, identity, and ethics

Mass sampling introduces two coupled problems: repeated measures require linking sessions to participants, and the entire design promise is anonymity. The resolution is a pseudonymous participant identity: on first visit, the client mints a random token, stored locally in the browser and attached to every contributed session. Sessions group by participant — necessary, since calibration-across-repetition is the thesis — while the token links to nothing: no name, no e-mail, no IP, no account. Its weakness is per-browser scope, complemented by an optional self-chosen codename for cross-device continuity; the failure mode of a forgotten codename splits one participant into two, biasing toward *under*-counting repetition — the conservative direction.

Contribution is opt-in, post-hoc, and honest: after the report, participants may download their own session unconditionally, and may separately contribute it to the research dataset under consent microcopy stating exactly what is sent (the download's contents, nothing more). Sessions by the developer and testers carry a pilot flag and are excluded from any analysis pool by pre-registered rule, which — together with the parameter hash — kills the residual circularity: the instrument is calibrated against ground truth, and its inventor is merely its first data point, with a known ceiling above him.

The regulatory posture was worked out with some care, because it is where informal projects most often compromise themselves. The trigger for human-subjects oversight is not funding, hosting, or institutional servers; it is intent to produce generalizable knowledge, and institutional jurisdiction attaches to the researcher's affiliation regardless of where the instrument lives. Bug-testing with a friend is product testing and implicates nothing. Public collection intended for publication is research, and the determination — for a design this benign, plausibly "not human subjects research" or a standard exemption — must be in hand *before* collection, because it cannot be applied retroactively to data already gathered. The project therefore sequences: private pilot now; institutional inquiry before any public launch; public collection only after determination. The ethics question is thereby made modular — the option to publish stays open without ever becoming an obligation.

### 9. Limitations

The instrument measures discrimination on one task family: direction of a one-dimensional trajectory over a fixed short horizon, from a fixed observation window, among four mechanism classes disclosed in advance. Generalization to richer perceptual settings — multivariate series, variable horizons, undisclosed mechanism families — is an empirical question, not an assumption. Ecological validity with respect to markets is bounded twice over: by reflexivity, which the current mechanisms do not model, and by the disclosure constraint itself, since real markets never tell the observer what fraction of their motion is martingale. The quadrant thresholds are provisional conventions awaiting population data. The oracle ceilings are exact only for the implemented generators; any parameter change re-opens them, which is why the harness and the parameter hash are governance, not tooling. And the single most important caveat is the instrument's own: at the session lengths individuals will realistically play, most classifications are provisional, and the C quadrant exists precisely to say so out loud.

### 10. Future work

Three extensions are parked, deliberately unimplemented. A feedback-learning mode — revealing each trial's mechanism immediately — converts the filter into a training apparatus and tests the original Feynman question directly: can the rules of this game be learned, and at what rate, by whom? Machine baselines — a Kalman filter, a particle filter, a small recurrent network — run through the identical protocol on the identical seeds place human and machine observers on one axis, which was the project's stated ambition from its first paragraph. An adversarial mode, in which the generative mechanism adapts against successful predictors, imports reflexivity into the laboratory and marks the honest boundary of any transfer claim toward markets. Beyond these, the instrument is a natural classroom laboratory in scientific inference — students watching the ball, registering hypotheses, and receiving calibration reports — and its population data would feed the same empirical program as the staged-exam framework it descends from: does the filter produce measurable diagnostic separation, and do profiles persist?

### 11. Conclusion

The project set out to predict a market and ended by building the thing that should have existed first: an instrument that asks, before any prediction is credited, whether the predictor can tell structure from noise at all — under disclosure, against a measured ceiling, with the instrument's own validity independently checked, and with "I have no edge" available as a skilled and scoreable answer. Its development enforced its thesis at every step. The founding intuition was treated as the first hypothesis and promptly wounded. The tuning was done against ground truth specifically to prevent the inventor from becoming the standard. The first empirical results were the instrument catching its own failures. And the first human classification it issued was a D, delivered to its author, held provisional exactly as its own rules require. The only thing more dangerous than a forecaster is a lucky forecaster; the only durable answer to either is an instrument that makes luck exhaust itself in public, one calibrated call at a time.
