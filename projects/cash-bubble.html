Abstract

The language of personal finance is saturated with fluid metaphors—cash flow, liquid assets, financial liquidity—yet these terms remain purely figurative. In this paper, a rigorous mathematical framework is developed in which personal finance operates as an actual dynamical system governed by conservation laws analogous to fluid mechanics.

Cash accounts are modeled as interconnected reservoirs of incompressible fluid, where the fundamental balance equation dV/dt = Q_in - Q_out directly parallels the continuity equation for mass conservation. The central novelty lies in treating volatile investments as compressible gas bubbles suspended within this financial fluid: market price acts as external pressure, unrealized gains represent stored potential energy, and realized gains correspond to thermodynamic work extracted during expansion.

This "cash bubble" hypothesis yields the First Law of Thermodynamics for investment accounts, establishes theoretical bounds on investment efficiency analogous to Carnot limits, and—through a consistency requirement—predicts that market volatility should scale with the square of price. Signal dynamics (velocity, acceleration, jerk of net worth) are further developed as diagnostic tools for detecting financial instability, and it is shown that common financial decision-making implicitly implements PID control. The framework unifies concepts from fluid mechanics, thermodynamics, and control theory into a coherent model of personal financial dynamics.

Overview

Key Concepts

  • Cash as Incompressible Fluid: Money in accounts behaves like water in connected tanks, governed by conservation laws
  • Investments as Compressible Bubbles: Market-dependent asset values exhibit thermodynamic properties—pressure (market price), potential energy (unrealized gains), and work (realized returns)
  • Signal Dynamics: The velocity and acceleration of net worth provide diagnostic signals for financial health and stability
  • Control Theory Connection: Personal financial decision-making implicitly implements PID (proportional-integral-derivative) control systems

Novel Results

  • First Law of Thermodynamics applied to investment accounts
  • Carnot-like efficiency bounds for investment returns
  • Volatility-price scaling prediction: σ ∝ p²
  • Connection between financial stability analysis and dynamical systems theory

Technical Approach

The framework begins with the fundamental balance equation for a single account—a direct application of mass conservation from fluid mechanics. This is extended to multi-account systems with transfers between accounts, creating a system of coupled differential equations. The dynamics of cash accounts (incompressible, linear) are then contrasted with investment accounts (compressible, nonlinear), revealing why traditional budgeting tools fail to capture investment volatility.

Signal dynamics are introduced as the first, second, and third time derivatives of net worth: velocity (rate of net worth change), acceleration (rate of change of velocity), and jerk (rate of change of acceleration). These metrics provide early warning indicators of financial distress and enable predictive control strategies.

The thermodynamic structure of the bubble hypothesis is developed through analogy with ideal gas law: market capitalization relates to pressure times volume in a gas, with the key insight that market volatility (uncertainty in pressure) should scale quadratically with price level—a prediction tested against empirical data from multiple asset classes.

Applications

  • Personal financial forecasting and stress-testing
  • Early detection of unsustainable spending patterns
  • Principled approach to investment allocation and rebalancing
  • Understanding the dynamical stability of household finances
  • Foundations for household-level economic modeling

Related Work

The historical precedent is the MONIAC (Monetary National Income Analogue Computer), a 1949 hydraulic model of national economy using flowing colored water. This project extends that concept from macroeconomic to household level, with rigorous mathematical development and modern computational implementation.

The work connects classical fluid mechanics, thermodynamics, and dynamical systems theory to personal finance—domains that have previously remained disconnected in academic literature.
